Fun things to try:

A demonstration of how much the markov chain can memorize:
Use Word Tokenization with length 6.  Click calculate longest deterministic chain.  Click the first entry in the list.  Keep clicking Generate until the top continuations has something other than 100%.  This whole passage is word-for-word a copy of the source material.  In other words, no creativity.  When we look at groups of 6 words to predict the next word, there becomes very very few unique outcomes.

A demonstration of spelling with a super small model:
Use Character Tokenization with length 4.  This model can only see the last 4 characters when guessing the next 1 character.  That means lots of time it can't even see whole words.  Even so, it does a pretty good job of spelling long words correctly.  For very long words, it makes up plausable looking new words sometimes.  You can see more or less made up words by setting the temperature higher up to Random or lower down to Deterministic.

A demonstration of robust generation:
Use Byte Pair Encoding tokenization.  BPE (Byte Pair Encoding) uses a dynamic method to extract out frequently used sequences, such as "ing" and "sh", and using them as a single concept.  This is more flexible than Word tokenization, because it can encode and create new words like "rerunningingly" that were not seen in the source material.  But unlike Character tokenization, it encode more information per token.  Use BPE with token length 8 and train it.  We can see from the message that the branching per state is low, which means the output will tend to not have much variety.  But if we use "Calculate longest deterministic chain", we can see that the chains are not rediculously long.  Certainly not like Word tokenization.  Now when we click "Use Random State" and then spam click "Generate (+20)", let's look at the Top 20 continuations.  We can see we rarely get to a situation with 100% for just 1 continuation.  This means it doesn't get stuck in a rut of copying the source material.

Play with temperature:
Try any of the above with a temperature other than the default "reserved".  What the temperature does is change the next token is selected.  The "Typical (1.0)" means to select the next token using exactly the same frequency that is observed in the source material.  For example, if text is "yesterday h" and we are looking for the next characters, we will see in the source material that maybe "a" appears 2 times and "i" appears once.  When selecting the next characters, "a" will be randomly selected 2/3 times, and "i" 1/3 times.  If we change the temperature all the way down to "Deterministic (0.0)" then we will always pick the most frequent next letter.  In this case "a".  If we move the temperature up, then the selection becomes more even, close to even odds for all next tokens.  What this feels like when you read the output text is interesting.  A fun starting point is Character tokenization with length 5. Try both extremes.  At what point does punctuation start to come up regularly or too much.  At what point are new words invented.
